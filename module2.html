Step 2 • Build a Stronger Secret

Long, memorable, and difficult to guess.

A passphrase combines several unrelated words into a longer credential. Length and unpredictability matter more than simply replacing letters with obvious symbols. Never use the examples on this page as your real password.

Step 3 • Break the Reuse Chain

One stolen password should not unlock your whole life.

When the same credential is reused, a breach at one service can become an attack on many accounts. Attackers may automatically test exposed credentials on email, social media, shopping, gaming, and school platforms.

1. One site is breachedA credential is exposed or stolen.
2. Credentials circulateThe email and password may be shared or sold.
3. Attackers reuse themThe same login is tested on other services.
4. Another account opensReused credentials allow unauthorized access.
5. Damage spreadsMessages, files, contacts, or recovery options may be abused.

Step 4 • Add Another Layer

Multi-factor authentication asks for more than one proof.

MFA combines two or more different factors. Even if someone learns your password, the additional factor can make unauthorized access more difficult.

01

Enable MFA first on high-value accounts

Prioritize email, school, banking or payment, cloud storage, and social-media accounts.

02

Protect backup codes

Store recovery or backup codes securely. Do not post, message, or photograph them carelessly.

03

Never share verification codes

A legitimate support person should not ask you to reveal a one-time authentication code.

Step 5 • Prepare for Recovery

Security includes getting back in safely.

Recovery methods should help you regain access without becoming an easy path for someone else. Keep them current, private, and protected.
